FlexiTac / recent work

A first tactile glove that measures normal force.

Version one puts force-sensitive resistors on a white service glove, routes their signals over the hand to an ESP32, and records pressure as a time-aligned force stream. The working prototype is now on a hand, sensing touch in real time.

03 / sensing principle

Pressure changes resistance. The circuit makes it readable.

An FSR becomes one half of a voltage divider. When normal force increases, its resistance changes; the ESP32 ADC sees the resulting voltage.

Why FSR for V1

Direct enough to learn from the hand.

FSRs do not provide a full pressure image, but they make normal force observable at key points. That is the right first signal for contact onset, grip loading and release.

04 / signal path

From contact to a normal-force record.

01

Press

A fingertip or palm presses against an object. The force is normal to the contact surface.

02

Sense

The FSR’s resistance shifts with the load, moving the analog divider voltage.

03

Sample & record

The ESP32 reads every channel and writes a timestamped force stream for the demonstration.

What V1 measures: normal force at selected contact points. It does not yet resolve shear direction, full contact geometry or distributed pressure across the entire hand.

06 / current limitations

V1 makes force visible, with deliberate trade-offs.

These are the constraints to resolve as the glove moves from a prototype toward reliable tactile capture.

01

Sensor orientation

An FSR is most informative when its sensing face is aligned with the contact normal. Glove motion and placement can change that orientation, affecting the measured force.

02

Limited contact points

Five discrete sensors capture selected locations, not the continuous contact field of full skin. Contact between sensors is currently unobserved.

07 / next-generation sensor

From wired FSRs to a printed flex sensor.

In parallel, FlexiTac's rectangular sensor is being reshaped into a hand — a flexible printed grid that replaces the discrete FSRs with dense, repeatable contact points.

Current EasyEDA design

A matrix in the shape of a hand.

Rows and columns of copper electrodes, sized to the glove, form a grid of touch points across the fingers and palm — the manufacturing-grade successor to the FSR layout.
